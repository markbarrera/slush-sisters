# The case review

Written 2026-08-04.

Five analysts were pointed at this business at once and told to disagree with
each other. A market and unit-economics analyst. A brand and positioning
strategist. An operations analyst wearing a red team hat. A growth and
category-creation analyst told to ignore every current constraint. And an
affirmative debater whose only job was to take the strongest case against and
beat it.

They produced about 130,000 words between them and they did not agree.

This document is the argument, not the verdict. Every position here has a
counter and the counter is on the page next to it. Where the evidence settles a
point, it says so. Where it doesn't, it says that too — and it says what one
phone call or one afternoon would cost to find out.

**Nothing has been rejected.** A few things have been re-sequenced, which is a
different thing and is marked as such.

---

## How to read this

Each debate is laid out the same way:

- **The motion** — the thing being argued about, in one sentence
- **For** — the strongest case, stated as strongly as it can honestly be stated
- **Against** — the same
- **Where it lands** — what the evidence actually supports today
- **What would settle it** — the cheapest thing that would end the argument

"Where it lands" is a reading of the evidence, not a decision. The decisions
are collected at the end, and most of them are Mark's.

Some terms used throughout, defined once:

- **BYOB / dry hire** — the customer buys and owns the alcohol; the vendor
  rents equipment and never sells or pours a drop. This is how every margarita
  machine rental company in Texas operates.
- **COI** — certificate of insurance. A one-page document proving you carry
  liability coverage. Venues and companies ask for it.
- **TABC** — Texas Alcoholic Beverage Commission, the state agency that
  regulates alcohol.
- **Dram shop** — the body of law about when a business that supplied alcohol
  is liable for what a drinker later does.
- **Contribution** — revenue minus the costs that only exist because the
  booking happened. What's actually left over per party, before fixed costs.

# Part 1 — The debates

## Debate 1 — Is $27,000 a year the ceiling?

**The motion:** the business tops out around $27,000 of annual revenue, which
makes everything downstream a margin fix rather than a growth strategy.

**For.** One machine, held overnight because next-morning pickup is a selling
point, serves one event per calendar day. Weather bounds the Texas season to
roughly March–October for kids' parties. After holidays, family commitments,
sports, illness, vacations and machine downtime, a realistic year is 60–80
events. Even the aggressive case — two machines, two drops per Saturday, all
year — lands near $27,000 and requires about 300 hours of adult driving, setup
and cleaning. That is a second job, and two independent methods (a bottom-up
market estimate and the site's own search arithmetic) both land at 24–36
bookings a year for the current setup.

**Against.** Do the division. $27,000 ÷ $250 = 108 events. Fifty-two weeks ×
two weekend nights = 104 nights. **The "market ceiling" is, to within rounding
error, the annual calendar of a single machine.** That is not a statement about
demand; it is a capacity utilization schedule for the asset the business
happens to own, presented as a fact about customers. And a machine is a
purchase order — used commercial dual-tank units run roughly $1,200–$3,000 and
return their cost in six to twelve rentals. A San Antonio operator running this
exact model has 25 machines, two vans, $250,000 of gross revenue and a $375,000
asking price. They did not find a bigger market. They bought more machines.

**Where it lands.** The affirmative wins the arithmetic and the negative wins
the constraint. Both of these are true at once:

- $27,000 was never a demand number. Roughly 25,000–35,000 affluent households
  sit within twenty minutes. At a deliberately conservative 4% hosting one
  machine-worthy event a year, that is 1,000–1,400 events of category demand —
  $300,000–$420,000 — before a single school or office event is counted. Demand
  is not the binding constraint and never was.
- The binding constraint is **contiguous adult weekend hours**. Machines are
  cheap; Saturdays are not. Every scale path has to pass one test: *does this
  add hours to a hired adult, or to a child?* If a child, it self-terminates
  around age fourteen.

Relieve the machine and the driver — both purchasable — and the same business
in the same territory models to roughly **$150,000–$250,000 within three
years.** That is a 6–9x correction and it is arithmetic, not optimism. It is
also, honestly, a mundane correction: it fixes a capacity mistake. It does not
prove a category exists. Those are separate arguments and Debate 12 handles the
second one.

**What would settle it.** A written list of turned-away Saturdays. Nothing
else. Until bookings are being declined for lack of a machine, "buy another
machine" is a guess; the day there are three names on that list it stops being
one.

## Debate 2 — Is $250 the right price?

**The motion:** the base price should go up.

**For.** ATX Marg Rentals charges **$350** for a functionally identical
dual-tank package with the same fifteen-mile radius, and without the candy
garnish, the next-morning pickup, or the story. Party Planet lists a
machine-only package at **$185** and the same machine *with a certified
bartender* at **$485** — so the Austin market clears the same physical object
anywhere across a 162% spread. Against a $485 ceiling, $250 is sitting near the
floor of its own band. A $75 increase across thirty bookings is roughly $2,250
a year of pure margin at zero cost and zero risk. Median household income runs
around $165,000 in Lakeway. This is not a market optimizing $250 down to $185.

**Against.** Two counters, and the first is stronger than it looks.

First: raising price is only free if nothing else changes. The site currently
tells a story that justifies a *lower* price as easily as a higher one — two
kids, a family business, supporting them is part of what you're buying.
Customers who book partly out of goodwill are the ones most likely to notice a
40% increase, and goodwill is not a renewable resource.

Second: the price umbrella is $300, not $2,000. The $2,000–$2,800 mobile-bar
figure that makes the adult market look enormous buys glassware, ice, mixers,
beer and wine service, liquor liability insurance, and someone to card the
nineteen-year-old cousin. Slush Sisters supplies none of that. Anchoring to it
sets prices that will not close.

**Where it lands.** Both sides agree the price should go up; they disagree
about how far. The floor of the range everyone supports is **$300–$325**. The
top of the defended range is $350, and $400–$450 for a dedicated adult tier
capped at around sixty guests. Nobody defended anything above $485 for this
package, because at $485 the customer can buy a machine *and* a certified
bartender from an established operator.

The counter about goodwill is real but survivable — the fix is to raise the
price and simultaneously make the package visibly better (garnish bar, second
flavor included, the flavor card), so the customer is buying more, not paying
more.

**This is Mark's call.** Price changes are on the dad side of the line in
`CLAUDE.md`, and this one is worth more per minute of thought than anything
else in this document.

## Debate 3 — Can this machine replace a bar?

**The motion:** a dual-tank frozen drink machine is a bartender replacement for
large parties.

**Against, first, because it is the more concrete case.** A dual-tank
commercial machine holds roughly 6–6.5 gallons across both tanks — about 100
eight-ounce servings, both flavors combined. Set that against real demand:

| Event | Drinks wanted | One fill covers |
|---|---|---|
| Kids' birthday, 25 kids × 2 | 50 | **200%** |
| Office party, 60 guests × 3 | 180 | ~55% |
| Corporate party, 100 guests × 3 | 300 | ~33% |
| Wedding, 150 guests × 4 | 600 | ~17% |

The machine is correctly sized for a children's birthday and undersized for
every adult event in the pitch. Refilling means hauling pre-batched cocktail —
24 gallons is about 200 pounds of liquid — in coolers, at a safe temperature,
poured warm into a chilled tank mid-party. That is a food-handling operation,
not an equipment rental. And it serves *two* batched drinks; a party wants beer,
wine, and something for the person who doesn't want a margarita.

**For.** Tank volume is the wrong metric. These are refrigeration units with a
hopper feeding a freezing cylinder, not coolers — throughput is hourly, not
per-fill. A Frosty Factory 215F produces 155 drinks per side per hour, 310 an
hour across both. Over four hours that is over a thousand servings from one
machine. Rental companies instruct customers to mix the next batch during the
event and feed it in as the first depletes; refill mid-party is the documented
normal operating procedure, not an edge case. Reventals — a company with a
commercial interest in selling you *more* machines — recommends one machine for
up to 100 guests. The 300-drink premise also assumes every guest drinks only
frozen margaritas, which describes no party that has ever happened.

**Where it lands.** The throughput rebuttal is correct on the physics and it
does not fully answer the objection, because throughput assumes somebody is
there mixing and feeding batches. That somebody is the thing the pitch says
you're replacing.

Which produces the most useful single reframe in the whole review, and both
sides can live with it:

> **It is not a bar. It is the best station at a party that already has one.**

That framing fixes three problems at once. It is truthful about capacity. It
stops the fight with established mobile bar companies. And it puts a bartender
in the room doing the gatekeeping, which quietly resolves half of Debate 4.

There is a second finding here that cuts against the "go bigger" instinct. The
150-guest tier produces more revenue at *no better hourly rate* — about $60 an
hour blended either way — with five extra hours of an adult standing at a party
at 9pm on a Saturday. **The moment you staff the event to keep the tanks full,
you have re-hired the bartender you were replacing.** The unattended 50–60
guest tier is the better business.

**What would settle it.** One event. Run a single 60-person adult party and
measure the actual consumption rate and the actual refill burden.

## Debate 4 — Is there real dram shop exposure?

**The motion:** renting a machine that ends up full of margaritas creates legal
liability if a guest later causes harm.

**Against exposure (the affirmative's case).** Texas Alcoholic Beverage Code
§2.01 defines a "provider" as *a person who sells or serves an alcoholic
beverage under authority of a license or permit … or who otherwise sells an
alcoholic beverage to an individual.* Slush Sisters does not sell alcohol, does
not serve alcohol, and holds no permit. It rents refrigeration equipment loaded
with non-alcoholic mix and drives away. **It is not a provider, so Chapter 2
does not reach it** — the exclusivity question never arises because there is no
provider. TABC's own guidance is that no permit is required to allow people to
bring their own alcohol where nothing is being sold. And the empirical check:
if this theory of liability were right there would be no margarita machine
rental industry in Texas. There are at least seven operators in the Austin
metro and one in San Antonio that has run this model for 42 years.

**For exposure (the red team's case).** §2.03 makes Chapter 2 the *exclusive*
remedy for providing alcohol — but only for persons **eighteen or older**. For
minors there is no exclusivity: §2.02(c) social host liability is live,
ordinary negligence is live, and §106.06 (making alcohol available to a minor)
is a criminal offence. Now put that beside the product: an unattended,
self-serve alcohol dispenser, with no ID check and no gatekeeper, at an event
where teenagers are plausibly present — a wedding, a family holiday party, a
graduation. The machine cannot tell a fifteen-year-old from a forty-five-year-
old and it does not stop pouring.

And there is a compounding fact nobody had connected: **next-morning pickup
means the machine stays at the customer's house overnight.** At a kids' party
that's a tank of limeade. At an adult event it is a tank of cocktails left
unattended in a house overnight, possibly with teenagers in it, possibly with
the host asleep.

**Where it lands.** The §2.01 reading is strong and probably right — the
statute says *sells*, and there is no sale. Forty-two years of an industry
operating this way without the theory being tested is meaningful evidence.

But the minor-exposure point does not depend on Chapter 2 at all, which is why
the §2.01 argument doesn't dispose of it. Social host liability attaches to the
*homeowner*, not to Slush Sisters — the counter-counter is right that the
machine doesn't create that exposure and its absence wouldn't remove it. A
tequila bottle on a kitchen counter is equally unsupervised.

What survives from the red team is not a legal conclusion. It is an operational
one, and it is unarguable:

> **Same-night pickup, mandatory, on any event with alcohol in the tank.**

That costs several hours of adult evening time and should be priced in, and it
is the right call regardless of how the statutory question resolves.

**What would settle it.** One hour with a Texas attorney on the §2.01 provider
question. Bundle it with the child labor question in Debate 5 and the chapters
question in Debate 12 so it is one hour, not three. **Mark's call.**

## Debate 5 — Can the girls be there when there's alcohol in the machine?

**The motion:** §106.09 keeps the two owners of the company out of the events
the adult business depends on.

**For the restriction.** TABC §106.09(a): *"No person may employ a person under
18 years of age to sell, prepare, serve, or otherwise handle liquor."* The
exceptions cover wine-only package stores and licensed on-premises
establishments; neither applies. "Otherwise handle" is broad language. At a
birthday party the alcohol goes in after the girls leave, or isn't there at
all. At an adult event the liquor is in the tank the whole time, and a
ten-year-old operating a dispenser containing tequila is handling liquor. TABC
will not parse LLC membership to decide whether "employ" applies. **Which means
the adult business earns its revenue at events the two owners of the company
legally cannot attend.**

**Against the restriction.** Read the text again. It governs *employment to
handle liquor* and says nothing about presence. "Liquor" is a defined term
(§1.04) — an alcoholic beverage other than a malt beverage above 5% ABV. It is
not a synonym for "any room where alcohol might later appear." A machine
containing citrus mix and no ethanol is not liquor; loading it, plugging it in
and leaving is not handling liquor. So the compliant model is not a workaround,
it's the plain operating sequence: the girls set up a machine containing only
non-alcoholic mix, in daylight, before the event starts. They arrange the
garnish bar, hand over the flavor card, photograph it, and leave. The customer
adds spirits after departure. Separately, Texas Labor Code §51.003 exempts a
child working in a nonhazardous occupation under a parent's direct supervision
in a business the parent owns or operates.

**Where it lands.** Both sides arrive at the same operating rule from opposite
directions, which is the most useful outcome in the document:

> **The girls set up in daylight with non-alcoholic mix and leave. Nobody under
> 18 is present once spirits are in the tank.**

The red team calls this a limitation and the affirmative calls it the plain
reading of the statute. It doesn't matter which framing you prefer — the
behaviour is identical either way, and it is not in dispute.

There is a real disagreement left underneath, and it belongs to Debate 8: does
that limitation hollow out the brand asset, or not?

There is also a genuine unresolved wrinkle worth an attorney's time. Federal
law (29 CFR 570.126) makes the parental exemption available only where the
business is **solely owned by the parent** — and this LLC is owned by the
children. Two readings: either they are owners rather than employees, so child
labor law doesn't attach at all; or the federal exemption is simply
unavailable. Texas's "owned *or operated by*" is more forgiving and likely
carries the day. Nothing is currently wrong. It is cheap to structure correctly
now and expensive to unwind later. Entangled with it: whether minors can
validly sign the customer agreements this business uses.

## Debate 6 — Does the adult business threaten the insurance?

**The motion:** marketing to adult events can void the coverage protecting the
children's business.

**For the risk.** Standard commercial general liability policies contain a
liquor liability exclusion. There's a carve-out — host liquor coverage — and it
applies *only if the insured is not in the business of manufacturing,
distributing, selling, serving, or furnishing alcoholic beverages.* Today,
Slush Sisters rents a machine that makes limeade; the carve-out is available
and coverage is cheap. Marketed as a cocktail service for adult events, an
insurer has a straightforward argument that the business **is** in the business
of furnishing alcohol. Host liquor coverage also does not apply where a charge
is made for the alcohol, and "charge" is read broadly — a "cocktail package"
price looks like a charge even if no bottle was ever bought.

Here is the part that makes this the most under-appreciated risk in the review,
and it is not dramatic. It is: run corporate events for a season with no
incident → then a routine accident at a kids' birthday, someone trips on a
power cord → the carrier reviews the account, finds the business publicly
marketed as a cocktail service, and denies on the liquor exclusion or material
misrepresentation. **The cost of the adult business lands on the children's
business, a year later, with no warning, uninsured.**

**Against the risk.** The standard form cuts the other way. The **2013 ISO CGL
revision added express language** clarifying that permitting a person to bring
alcoholic beverages onto the premises for consumption there — fee charged or
not — is not by itself the business of selling, serving or furnishing alcohol.
The form was amended specifically to protect BYOB operators, which is exactly
this posture. General liability plus liquor liability for a dry-hire operation
runs roughly $500–$1,500 a year, which is affordable and not the issue.

**Where it lands.** The affirmative is right about the form and the red team is
right about the mechanism. An insurer can price and word a policy however it
likes, and the 2013 revision protects an operator who *permits* alcohol — not
necessarily one whose website is selling cocktails.

Which means both sides converge on the same instruction, and it is the single
highest-value item in this entire document:

> **Describe the business to the agent in writing — exactly as it will operate,
> customer-supplied alcohol, adult events — and get the answer in writing
> before anything is marketed. If the carrier says no, that is the decision,
> made for free.**

One phone call. Zero dollars. It answers more of this review than any other
single action, and it has to happen before the adult page goes live, not after.

Both sides also agree on the free half of the mitigation: **the marketing
language is legally load-bearing.** Write copy that describes renting a
machine. Never copy that describes providing cocktails. That distinction costs
nothing and does real work.

**Mark's call.** Insurance is money and legal.

## Debate 7 — One brand, or two?

**The motion:** the adult business should run under a separate name.

**For two brands.** Disney created Touchstone Pictures in 1984 for exactly this
reason: protect the family brand while releasing adult-oriented work. It's the
canonical precedent and it's what a cautious advisor reaches for. And there is
a live cautionary case — SunnyD's 2023 vodka seltzer, launched in packaging
near-identical to the lunchbox drink, drew a formal complaint to the Distilled
Spirits Council and an analyst's summary of the problem: *"It is very hard if
you have a brand built on a loyal followership of younger people to then stretch
that brand into alcohol."* Chuck E. Cheese serves beer and wine at 75–80% of
locations and pays a permanent low-grade reputational tax for it. Girl Scouts —
the most sophisticated child-fronted brand in America — sends cease-and-desist
letters to breweries over cookie *names*, treating even a name association with
alcohol as worth legal spend.

**For one brand.** The stated goal is the disqualifier: *the money isn't in the
rentals, it's in the brand, and the brand is them.* A separate adult brand is,
by construction, revenue that builds no brand — the one thing the strategy
explicitly says not to optimise for, dressed up as risk management. Every
corporate dollar would build equity in a name nobody knows, requiring a second
site, a second Google Business Profile, a second review base, a second SEO
effort in a market worth ninety searches a month.

The Disney precedent also doesn't transfer, for one structural reason:
**Disney's adult product was content Disney made.** Slush Sisters' adult product
is an empty machine someone else fills. Touchstone existed because Disney was
*authoring* the R-rated thing. Same with SunnyD — that failure is about
authoring an alcohol product, not about serving adults. And the category has
already settled this: Margarita Man Houston, and every other operator, runs
kids' parties and corporate events under one name with segmented pages. Kona
Ice does school fundraisers and corporate catering under one brand across 2,500
trucks and nobody is confused.

**Where it lands.** One brand, segmented pages, with the message order inverted
by audience:

- **Kids' page:** founders → candy garnish → flavors → price → logistics
- **Adult page:** capability → price → insured/LLC/paperwork → logistics →
  *then* founders, once, late

The existing `/margarita-machine-rental-austin` page already does this, and the
line near its bottom — *"It is a real business and they run it — which is either
a fun detail or the whole reason you book us"* — gives the buyer permission to
not care, which is precisely what makes them care. That page is the template.

The two-brand case is not rejected. It is **held as a fallback with a trigger**:
if the adult offer demonstrably stalls and the post-mortems point at the name —
not before — go endorsed rather than separate: *"Chilled — by Slush Sisters."*
Built speculatively, that signals they themselves think there's a problem,
which invites the buyer to agree. Built on evidence, it's a fix.

A genuinely separate brand is only justified if they hire non-family staff and
run a distinct operation. Names recorded to keep the option open, not to open
it: *Bee Cave Batch Co.*, *620 Frozen*, *Hill Country Batch*.

**Names to refuse.** Anything welding the kid brand to alcohol in one
searchable string: "Slush Sisters After Dark," "Happy Hour," "Cocktail Co.,"
"Sister-Ritas," anything with "Bar." Each creates a permanent artifact a
brand-safety screen will surface. "Cocktail Service" is additionally wrong on
the facts, because nobody is serving.

**Naming the offer instead of a brand.** "Slush" actively mis-describes a rocks
margarita or a batched punch, and that's a category-accuracy problem, not a
credibility one. Two axes, both under the master brand:

```
Slush Sisters
  Mode:  Frozen   |   Chilled        (what the machine does)
  Mix:   Classic  |   Fresh Press    (docs/product-tiers.md)
```

"Chilled" is descriptive, adult-legible, carries no alcohol coding, and is a
word an eight-year-old would use — which keeps it in voice. Page titles are
named by the job: "Office Holiday Parties," not a sub-brand.

## Debate 8 — Does the kid-founder story help or hurt with adult buyers?

**The motion:** two children as founders is an asset in the corporate and adult
market, not a liability.

**Against.** The office manager's actual job is **not to get blamed.** She is
not optimising for a great party; she is optimising against the scenario where
something goes wrong and it's traceable to her vendor choice. Alcohol is
already the highest-liability line item on any work party — every risk guide in
the category tells her to get a COI naming the company as additional insured,
verify licensing, and stipulate alcohol-awareness-trained staff. A vendor whose
principals are 8 and 11 makes that conversation weird. And there's an HR
dimension nobody wants to litigate on Monday: children present at an event where
employees are drinking. Even at zero legal exposure, the optics land on the
person who booked it.

**For.** She is buying one thing: **a party people talk about.** Her success
metric is "coworkers enjoyed it and said so." A story is not decoration on that
purchase, it is the product. "We booked two kids who run their own company" is
a better line at the party than anything a bartending service provides.

The precedent is strong. Moziah Bridges founded Mo's Bows at nine — bow ties
are an adult product with an adult purchase occasion — and sold $600,000+
through Cole Haan, Bloomingdale's, HSN and Neiman Marcus. **The kid-founder
story was not a liability at Neiman Marcus; it was the reason a commodity
accessory got shelf space.** Neiman Marcus is a more conservative buyer than a
forty-person Austin agency.

And there is no alternative differentiation available. Against a field of
competitors with Domain Ratings of 0–9 pulling under thirty organic visits a
month, "we're more professional" is not a claim this business can win.

**Where it lands.** It helps, conditional on two things.

**One: clear the professional floor first.** The story can't do its work until
the buyer has stopped worrying. The site already asserts the right thing —
*"Slush Sisters LLC is a registered Texas business with liability insurance."*
That sentence is worth more to a corporate buyer than any brand decision here.
It needs backing: a COI that can name a client as additional insured, a W-9, and
a one-page rental agreement. **That paperwork, not brand architecture, is the
actual unlock for the adult market.** (Mark's call — legal and money.)

**Two: segment the buyer, because "corporate" is not one buyer.**

| Buyer | Verdict |
|---|---|
| 200+ employees, with procurement and legal | Won't book. Vendor onboarding alone kills it. Don't chase |
| **15–60 person Austin companies** — startups, agencies, brokerages, dental and medical practices, law firms | **The target.** One person, a budget, no approval chain |
| Weddings | Genuinely mixed. Maximally risk-averse about the day, maximally story-motivated in planning. High effort per booking, and venue vendor lists are a real gate |

**Drop the word "corporate."** It imports procurement requirements this
business can't satisfy and doesn't need. The right phrase is **"office holiday
parties"** — same buyer, none of the baggage.

## Debate 9 — What is the actual reputational risk?

**The motion:** the reputational danger is the sentence "margarita machine run
by an eight-year-old."

**Against that framing.** That sentence is fine, and defensible in one line.
The failure mode is imagery and language, not the business model. The specific
thing that becomes a story is a photograph or clip of a child in frame with
visibly drinking adults at night, or a child narrating a cocktail recipe.
Decontextualised, that travels, and nothing about the LLC structure protects
against it.

The red team put it more sharply. Assemble what is already public by deliberate
decision: first names in use, school shirt visible, home bar in frame, service
area published. All correct decisions for a kids' business. All accelerants for
this one. A guest at a holiday party photographs the machine — branded with two
little girls, their first names, the candy garnish, the whole wholesome
apparatus — dispensing tequila cocktails at 9pm, posts it with a funny caption,
and it spreads *because it is genuinely funny*. The headline writes itself and
there is no correction to issue, because every fact in it is true.

And note the cruel mechanics: **the differentiator does the damage.** The candy
garnish exists to make the cup photogenic. The strategy is working exactly as
designed at the moment the photo gets taken.

**The counter, which is not a dismissal.** This risk is real and it is
manageable by policy rather than by abandoning the business:

- **Nothing shot at an adult event after dark. Ever.** This amends a decision
  recorded in `strategy.md` — "the home bar in frame is fine" is right for a
  *daytime shot of a stocked shelf at home*, where it reads as competence to an
  adult booker. It is wrong as a general rule, because it will get applied to
  event footage. **The rule should key on the event, not the bar.**
- **The girls never appear in adult-event content.** Not the photo, not the
  video, not the caption.
- **No recipe containing a spirit is ever published under the brand.**
  `product-tiers.md` proposes the girls develop a skinny margarita. Split it:
  they develop and film the *limeade*. The tequila ratio is a line on an adult
  handout given at setup. Never a video, never a page.

**The largest exposure, which nobody had priced.** Instagram and TikTok both
have alcohol content policies, and the accounts are parent-operated because
both girls are under thirteen — already a slightly precarious posture. But the
bigger issue is that **brand partnerships are where kid-creator money actually
is, and they run brand-safety screens.** A family-brand partnership manager
doing diligence will scroll the feed. Cocktail content fails that screen
instantly and permanently — the archive doesn't go away.

Stated plainly: the adult events business might make a few thousand dollars in
November and December while disqualifying the girls from the exact partnerships
the whole strategy exists to earn. That is the real price of alcohol proximity,
and it is paid in the currency the strategy says matters most.

**Where it lands.** Publish **the Tequila Refusal** (already in
`big-ideas.md`) — a plain policy page: the machine makes frozen limeade, adults
add their own tequila, and Slush Sisters will not take money from an alcohol
brand. Published early it's a principle. Published after a problem it's damage
control. It also pre-answers the only objection a school, a reporter or a brand
partner would ever raise.

One dissent worth recording: the red team argues the adult business makes that
page impossible to write. It doesn't — the page describes exactly what is true
in both cases. But it does mean the page has to be written *first*, and the
behaviour has to match it.

**Mark's call**, because it's a public commitment about where money won't come
from.

## Debate 10 — Is there a better answer to the idle-capacity problem?

**The motion:** filling November and December requires the alcohol business.

**For.** December is currently worth zero. The kids' birthday calendar goes
quiet, the machine sits in the garage, and corporate party season is roughly
the first three weeks of December — say 12–15 usable evenings. At $600 each
that's $7,000–$9,000 in six weeks, which is 40–50% of current annual revenue.
That is not a rounding error. The seasonality argument is the strongest leg of
the whole adult thesis: kids' frozen demand peaks June–August and dies in
December; adult chilled demand peaks October–December and April–May. **They are
close to inverse.** This does not require the adult market to be larger, only
differently timed — which is checkable and true.

**Against.** The idle capacity is real. The alcohol answer is not the only
answer, and three alternatives fill the same hole with none of the cost:

- **Corporate summer** — employee appreciation days, company picnics, July team
  events, intern days. Daytime, outdoors, Austin heat, no alcohol needed, **the
  girls can attend**, the frozen product is exactly right, and it's the same
  office manager with the same card. The adult pitch chases December because
  the calendar is empty; the better corporate business is June–August, where the
  product already fits and the brand is an asset rather than a hazard.
- **The Realtor Circuit** (already in `big-ideas.md`) — weekday afternoons
  currently worth nothing, adult buyers, repeat bookings, girls present, no
  insurance change, no permit question, no legal question.
- **Schools, churches, teacher appreciation weeks, sports banquets** — daytime,
  kids present, brand-reinforcing.

**Where it lands.** These are not competing answers, they're a sequence. Every
alternative above fills the same idle capacity, keeps the girls in the product,
requires zero regulatory change, and costs nothing to test. Do those first —
not instead.

The seasonality argument survives intact and is the reason the adult tier
doesn't get dropped: the alternatives above are mostly *spring and summer*
demand, and December stays empty without something adult-shaped in it.

**Nothing here is rejected. The order changed.** Test the daylight options
first, because they answer the "the machine is idle" premise for free, and
whatever December revenue is still wanted after that is then a smaller,
better-understood bet.

## Debate 11 — What business is this, actually?

This is the debate nobody was assigned and everybody arrived at.

**The reframe from the unit economics.** The dominant costs are per-*trip*, not
per-*guest*. Drive time, setup, breakdown and cleaning are roughly 85% of the
cost base and are nearly identical whether twenty or sixty people attend. Only
consumables scale.

So: the same ~4 hours of truck and family time yields about **$110 of
contribution at $250, and about $278 at $450.** That is a **2.5× improvement on
the scarcest input in the business.**

> **The adult play is not a bigger market. It is better yield on a fixed, small
> capacity.**

That is a materially different claim from the one the docs were making, and it
changes what you'd do. It also means market sizing was the wrong analysis
throughout. Both markets are orders of magnitude larger than one truck and one
driver can serve. The right question is yield per adult weekend hour.

**And then the finding that reframes it again.** The $250 flat rental is not a
price — it is a business model choice, and it is the *lowest-yield use of the
asset.*

Kona Ice runs the same physical logistics — a frozen beverage unit, a vehicle,
events — and did **$352 million across roughly 1,933 franchise trucks in 2024**,
about $182,000 per unit per year. Kona Ice does not rent machines. It **sells
cups at events** — schools, sports, festivals, fundraisers — and gives a share
back to the host organisation.

Apply that to the machine that already exists. A 300-person school carnival,
two hours, $3 a cup at a 60% take rate is roughly **$540 gross on one
machine-night**, against $250 for renting it out. A church festival, an HOA pool
party, a youth sports tournament, an elementary field day: same asset, same
truck, 2–3× the revenue, **no alcohol anywhere near it, and the girls can
actually be the ones serving.**

**The counter, which is real.** Serving cups is a food-service operation in a
way that dropping off a machine is not. HB 2844 took effect 1 July 2026 — all
mobile food vendors now license through DSHS statewide rather than through
local health departments. Type I (no temperature-controlled foods) runs about
$309; Type II about $618 plus a $400 pre-licensing inspection. Dispensing at a
public event almost certainly needs one, and public events with alcohol need a
permit holder to manage the alcohol, which the dry-hire model can't supply.
It's also a longer day and more adult hours than a drop-off.

**Where it lands.** This is the single highest-value unexploited idea in the
review, and it should be a **named second line, not a side effect**:

- Higher yield per machine-night than the rental model
- Zero alcohol adjacency, so no insurance question and no §106.09 question
- Fundraiser-shaped — schools say yes to a percentage-back offer from two local
  kids essentially every time
- Validated at ~2,000 units nationally by a company that built the entire model
- **The girls can front all of it**, which is the thing the alcohol business
  can never offer

The permit cost is a few hundred dollars and is knowable in an afternoon. That
is a low price for the only revenue line in this document that is
simultaneously higher-yield, brand-safe, and child-frontable.

## Debate 12 — Is there a category to create here?

**The motion:** this is a category-creation opportunity, not just a local
business.

**Against.** Three candidate categories were tested and are dead ends, and
saying so matters because naming a category that already has a name is as much
an error as under-reaching:

- *"Self-serve batched beverage for events"* — the industry already calls this
  **dry hire**. It's an established and growing line: dry hire runs 40–60%
  cheaper than staffed equivalents, and non-alcoholic and low-ABV is the
  fastest-growing segment, addressing a sober-curious cohort estimated at 35%
  of US adults. Ride the tailwind; there's no name to claim.
- *"Party in a box"* — in wide commercial use for decoration kits. Taken.
- *"Frozen drink machine rental"* — Margarita Man has franchised this exact
  business since 1984 across Houston, South Florida, Las Vegas and elsewhere.
  The category isn't vacant, it's just badly served locally. Winning Austin is
  an execution job.

**For.** There is one vacancy and it is real: **a commercial brand of real
businesses operated by children.** What exists today is:

- **Lemonade Day** — a nonprofit reaching over a million children across 80+
  licensed markets since 2007. It proves demand for the structure at scale, and
  it is a one-day curriculum, not an ongoing business.
- **Children's franchises** — Urban Air, The Little Gym, Snapology. Every one
  sells services *to* children, operated by adult franchisees. None is operated
  by children.
- **Kidpreneur education** — several curricula. All teach; none operates.

So the market has entrepreneurship education for kids and services for kids,
and **zero revenue-generating businesses operated by kids under a shared
brand.** That gap is genuine and Slush Sisters already occupies it as a single
instance.

**The name proposed: don't name an industry, name a standard.**

> **KID-RUN** — a certification mark applied to real businesses where children
> own the entity, do the customer-facing work, and publish their books.

Three reasons this is the strongest available version:

1. **It's defensible.** The retraction in `big-ideas.md` was right that there
   is no IP in a sweet on a cup rim. There *is* IP in a certification mark with
   a published standard, and it's the only asset in this business a competitor
   can't replicate with a shopping trip. The audit criterion is already
   invented — the **Open Ledger** is the standard.
2. **It turns Chapters from a legal problem into a legal non-problem.** Under
   the FTC Franchise Rule a franchise requires three elements: trademark
   association, significant control or assistance, **and a required payment of
   at least $500 within six months.** Remove the payment and the Rule does not
   attach, regardless of what the parties call it. A free licence with a rule
   set is the structurally correct design — and it happens to be the design
   already sketched in `big-ideas.md`.
3. **It makes the girls category-definitional rather than an entry in
   somebody's category.**

**The honest counter, which is not small.** A certification mark with one
certified business is a logo. Kid-Run is worth something at fifty chapters and
worth nothing at three, and getting to fifty requires two teenagers to sustain
interest through the years when nearly every teenager's interest goes
elsewhere. Two further caveats: Texas's Business Opportunity Act still needs a
lawyer's eye, and a certification mark's owner generally cannot use the mark on
its own services — meaning a separate entity likely holds it. Both are counsel
questions, not blockers.

**Where it lands.** Recorded as the highest-ceiling option and explicitly
**gated, not started.** It is a Layer 3 decision (see Part 2), and the gate is
an audience, not a date.

## Debate 13 — Two findings nobody was arguing about

Both surfaced sideways and both matter more than the debate they came out of.

**Fresh Press is a bigger regulatory step than the cocktail question.**
`product-tiers.md` proposes fresh-squeezed citrus and real fruit, prepared the
morning of the party. That moves the product from shelf-stable syrup toward a
potentially hazardous food prepared in a home kitchen, and **Texas cottage food
law does not cover it.** This is currently written up as a near-term plan and
nobody had flagged it. It needs checking before the first Fresh Press booking,
whichever direction the business goes.

The idea is not in trouble — the content play attached to it (reading the
ingredient list on the mix everyone else uses; squeezing a hundred limes on
camera; the freeze test) is still among the best material in the whole
programme, and the physics test in `product-tiers.md` still has to happen
first regardless. It just needs a permit question answered alongside the
freeze test.

**Every kid founder who scaled converted from service to product.** Five cases
were examined:

| Founder | Started at | What it became |
|---|---|---|
| **Mikaila Ulmer** (Austin) | 4 | Me & the Bees — ~600 stores incl. Whole Foods, Target, H-E-B; a Penguin Random House book; now runs it remotely as an Emory economics student |
| **Alina Morse** | 7 | Zolli Candy — $6M retail at 13; 25,000+ stores by 2025 |
| **Moziah Bridges** | 9 | Mo's Bows — still operating at 24; seven-figure NBA licensing deal; Neiman Marcus |
| **Ryan Kaji** | 3 | Ryan's World — ~$35M/yr, licensed IP, a feature film. Depended on 2015–18 YouTube conditions that no longer exist. Not a plan |
| **Maddie Bradshaw** | 10 | M3 Girl Designs — 1,000+ stores, then **closed in 2015** after suing small competitors over a design it had neither trademarked nor patented |

Four things the survivors did:

1. **They converted from service to product** — a physical thing with shelf
   life that ships without the founder present. Not one scaled the original
   hands-on service. **Slush Sisters is currently a service.**
2. **They got into physical retail early.** Distribution, not audience, was the
   scaling mechanism for three of the four.
3. **They separated the founder's labour from the revenue** — licensing, a
   co-packer, a distributor — before the founder turned sixteen.
4. **They had a cause or hook that outlived the "cute kid" window**, which
   closes around fourteen.

And the anti-pattern from M3: **do not litigate.** A kid business in a thousand
stores destroyed itself sending cease-and-desists over IP it did not own. The
garnish-licensing retraction already in `big-ideas.md` shows the right instinct
is present. Keep it.

**The honest base rate.** More than thirty children have pitched on Shark Tank.
A handful are running businesses of consequence a decade later. The great
majority stayed small or stopped, and the ones that stopped mostly stopped
quietly — which is why there's no clean dataset and why every "kidpreneur"
listicle is doing heavy survivorship-bias lifting. Assume the base rate for a
kid business reaching $1M+ in annual revenue is **low single-digit percent**.

The realistic target to aim at is **Mikaila Ulmer**, and it's worth being
specific about what that looks like at ten years: a nationally known founder, a
real product in ~600 stores, a book from a major publisher, a paid speaking
career, a scholarship, and a business she runs remotely from college. That is a
superb outcome. It is also probably a low-seven-figure enterprise, not Dollar
General.

# Part 2 — The strongest realistic plan

Three layers, strictly sequenced, with gates between them. The gates are the
important part: the failure mode for this family is not insufficient ambition —
the idea board has 101 cards on it. The failure mode is starting six things and
finishing none.

### Layer 1 — the cash engine (now → 18 months)

- **Fix the price.** $250 → $325 for the standard package. The market is
  $224–$350 for a worse package. Worth roughly $25,000/year at three-year
  volumes and costs nothing. *(Mark's call.)*
- **Make the phone calls that are free.** The insurance agent, in writing.
  Ten demand-testing calls to small Austin offices in September. Together they
  cost one evening and zero dollars and they answer most of Part 1.
- **Take the daylight adult business first** — realtor open houses, corporate
  summer, schools and churches. No permit question, no insurance change, girls
  present.
- **Add the non-alcoholic event line** (Debate 11). Sub-brand, per-cup or
  per-attendee, percentage back to the host organisation. Check the DSHS mobile
  food permit first.
- **Buy machine #2 when #1 turns away three bookings.** Not before. A written
  list, not a feeling.
- **Hire the first operator at ~15 rentals/month.** College students,
  $22–28/hour. The test for every growth decision from here: *does this add
  hours to a hired adult or to a child?* If a child, don't.
- **Then, and only then, one December test event.** One customer, small office,
  $500–$700, positioned as a station not a bar, dry hire, same-night pickup,
  girls absent, insurance confirmed in writing. Call it a test. No launch, no
  repositioning of the site.

**Gate to Layer 2:** 100 rentals completed, with documented unit economics.

### Layer 2 — the audience (6–36 months)

Three content franchises run relentlessly beats ten run occasionally. On
distribution logic, the three are:

- **Dad's Performance Review** — the funniest true thing about the company, and
  the one most likely to be shared by adults with reach.
- **Kids Review Ads** — aimed directly at marketers, who share compulsively,
  book corporate events, and decide brand partnerships.
- **The Open Ledger** — the credibility spine, and later the certification
  standard if Debate 12 ever runs.

Plus **the Annual Report**, mailed to forty people a year. About $60. The
cheapest press lottery ticket available and a physical object a journalist can
hold.

Email is the only metric that counts. Followers are rented; the list is owned.
Collect on the booking path, where the submitter is a parent — the fan path
stays pointed at the platforms, for the COPPA reasons in `strategy.md`.

**Gate to Layer 3:** 10,000 emails.

### Layer 3 — the transferable asset (decide at month 36, not now)

By month 36 the audience will have said which of these it pulls on. Pick
**one**:

- **A product with shelf life.** The garnish kit is the seed. All four scaled
  precedents did this and it is the highest-probability path to a real company.
  A national audience cannot rent a machine in Lakeway; it can buy a box.
- **The Kid-Run chapter network.** Free licence, no fee, no FTC Franchise Rule
  exposure, monetised through supply. Highest strategic ceiling, most legal
  groundwork, and the best fit for two teenagers who need to stop doing manual
  labour.
- **A book.** Penguin Random House published Mikaila's at fifteen. A book is a
  credential machine that converts into paid speaking — the only revenue line
  in this review that scales *with* the girls' time rather than despite it,
  which makes it the right shape at 17–22.

### The honest probability distribution

| Outcome over ten years | Probability |
|---|---|
| Quietly winds down when Harper is 14–16 | **40–50%** |
| A real $150–250K/year family business, regional brand, transformative on a college application | **25–40%** |
| $500K–$1M+, multiple markets or a product line, a genuine small company | **8–15%** |
| Mikaila Ulmer tier — national brand, book, speaking, retail distribution | **2–4%** |
| Category-defining, eight figures | **<1%** |

The modal outcome is that it ends. That's true of every business and it's true
here. It is not an argument against trying, because the expected value is
dominated by the tail and the downside is a few thousand dollars of machines
and a childhood spent doing something interesting.

### What has to be true, that nobody has priced

**Money and governance, before they're needed.** Texas has no child-influencer
trust law. Sixteen states have introduced them — California SB 764 requires 65%
of a minor's gross earnings into trust where the minor appears in ≥30% of
monetised content; Illinois since July 2024; Minnesota requires 100% for
under-14s; Utah applies above $150,000 of revenue.

**Set up the trust voluntarily and say publicly that you did.** It costs
little, it's the right thing, and it's the single most credibility-generating
governance move available — it makes the family unimpeachable on the one
question every journalist will eventually ask. *(Mark's call.)*

# Part 3 — The architecture that follows

This is the part Mark asked for: given all of the above, what shape should the
site be?

## The insight it's built on

The machine has **two tanks**.

A Lakeway backyard party on a Saturday afternoon is children running around
*and* parents standing near a cooler. It is not a kids' party or an adult
party. It is both, simultaneously — and **every competitor in this market
serves exactly half of it.**

One tank for the kids. One tank the grown-ups spike.

And the kid brand, which reads as a liability in the adult market, is in *this
setting* the qualifier. A margarita machine company selling into a party with
children present has an awkwardness problem. A company founded by an
eight-year-old and a ten-year-old, whose logo is a candy garnish, does not. The
"minors present" fact that the red team named as a risk is, at a family party,
the market — and the kid brand is the only credible way to serve it.

That is not a pivot. It is the current business finally charging for both
halves of the party it is already standing in.

**A caution to hold alongside it.** This works because a family backyard party
is a single event with two audiences. It does not extend to an office holiday
party at 9pm, which is a different room with different rules, and the
distinction has to stay visible in the site's structure. Where the two-tank
story is told, children are present and it's daylight. Where the adult offer is
sold, it's a different page with a different message order.

## This is a phone site

Before any of the page structure below: **nearly everyone arrives on a phone**,
from a bio link, a text message, a neighbourhood Facebook post, or a shared
story. Desktop is the exception. Every page gets designed at 390px first and
allowed to grow — see the mobile-first convention in `CLAUDE.md`.

One thing this immediately settles: **the three-part split below is not
navigation.** On a phone the nav collapses behind a ☰, so anything put there is
invisible until someone taps. Three doors in the nav would be three doors
nobody sees. The split lives in the **first screen of the homepage** — three
tappable cards, above the fold, each one a full-width target. The nav stays
short, and "Book" stays visible in the header at every width, outside the menu.

### Different doors for different arrivals

The same homepage has to serve people who arrive by very different routes and
want very different things. On a phone, in the first three seconds:

| They arrive from | They are | What they need first |
|---|---|---|
| **A social bio link** (Instagram, TikTok) | Curious about *the girls* — many not local, and many will never rent anything | The story, and something to follow or subscribe to. Not a booking form |
| **SMS / a text from a friend** | Already sold. Someone told them "book these kids" | The Book button, immediately. Nothing else matters |
| **A neighbourhood Facebook group** | Local, comparing options, mildly sceptical | Price, area, proof it's real. Then the story |
| **Google** ("margarita machine rental austin") | An adult with an event and a budget | Capability, price, insured/LLC. Founders last |
| **Press coverage** | The whole metro at once, briefly | Who they are, do you come to my part of Austin, and how do I get in touch |

Two implications worth building for:

- **Tag the links so the site can tell them apart.** Bio links and SMS links
  carry a `?from=` parameter; the page can lead with the right door. This costs
  a few lines of JavaScript and no extra pages. Start by tagging the links and
  reading the data — reorder the page only once there's evidence.
- **The social arrival is the most valuable and the worst served.** They are the
  audience the whole strategy is built to earn, they are mostly not local, and
  today they land on a page selling a machine they cannot rent. That is the
  single biggest mobile gap on the site.

## What's wrong with the site today

Five pages built around one product at one price for one audience, plus six
service-area pages and an SEO page bolted on. Specifically:

- The **homepage sells a machine**, when the thing that converts a stranger is
  two sisters who run a real company.
- There is **no route for the adult buyer** except the Austin SEO page, which
  is doing double duty as both a search landing page and the entire adult
  offer.
- The **cold-drink capability is invisible**, and it's the only capability in
  this market nobody advertises.
- **Nothing captures an email**, which `strategy.md` correctly identifies as
  the site's highest-value job and which it still does not do.
- There is **no page a reporter, brand manager or podcast booker can land on**
  and decide in eight seconds that this is real.
- `/about` is a footnote when the story is the product.

## Why this shape and not another

**Three doors on the homepage, not one funnel.** Three different people arrive
here — a parent booking a birthday, an adult booking an event, and someone who
found them through a video. Today all three land on a page selling a machine.
The most valuable of the three is the third, and the site currently has nothing
for them.

**The adult offer gets its own page, not its own brand.** Debate 7 settled
this. Same name, opposite message order, and the founders appear once, late,
where they give the buyer permission to not care.

**`/promise` is published before `/grown-ups`.** Non-negotiable, and it's the
cheapest insurance in this document. It also does commercial work: it
pre-answers the objection a school, a reporter or a brand-partnership manager
would otherwise raise.

**`/ledger` is a page, not a blog post.** It's the credibility spine of
everything in Part 2, it's the certification standard if Debate 12 ever runs,
and it's the thing that distinguishes this from every kid business that made
unverifiable claims.

**The service-area pages stay.** They work, they cost nothing to keep, and
they're the local search surface.

**What's deliberately not here:** a second brand, a shop, a chapters page, a
kit product. All of those are Layer 3 and all of them are gated on an audience
that doesn't exist yet. Building any of them now is the failure mode named at
the top of Part 2.

**The correction is a capacity correction, not a demand discovery.** The
$27,000 number was wrong in a mundane way — it treated a $1,500 asset and an
unpaid driver as fixed constants. Fixing that gets to roughly $250,000, not to
a category. The genuinely interesting part of the thesis — that *who runs it*
is a variable no competitor can turn — is unproven and rests on content
performance, which is the least predictable input anywhere in these documents.

**Almost every number in Part 2 is an estimate.** No invoice or booking data
exists in this repo. The cost structure is more reliable than the levels, the
market sizing rests on two unverified conversion assumptions, and the
three-year revenue model assumes execution that hasn't happened yet. The first
hundred real bookings will be worth more than all of this analysis.

**The precedent data cuts against the service business specifically.** Every
kid founder who scaled sold a *thing*. Slush Sisters sells *hours of a machine
in a truck*. Until there is a product, the ceiling is the number of Saturdays in
a year multiplied by the number of machines — and that is a number, however
large you make it, that a serious person can calculate in advance.

**And the category needs populating.** A certification mark with one certified
business is a logo.
