# Two products, not one

> **Read this first: the machine chills as well as freezes.** It is a drinks
> dispenser with two modes, not a slushie machine. Everything below is about
> the frozen side, but the cold side opens a second market — grown-up cocktails
> at holiday and corporate parties, cheaper than a bartender. See
> `big-ideas.md`. Cold mode also removes the alcohol ceiling that frozen mode
> imposes, so stronger drinks become possible. Carbonated drinks are the one
> real limit: dispense the base, top the glass with the fizz.

An idea worth taking seriously: adults and kids should not be drinking the same
thing, and should not be paying the same price.

Right now there is one product. One mix, one price, one recipe, whether it is a
six-year-old's birthday in Bee Cave or a grown-up party on Lake Austin. That is
leaving both money and quality on the table.

---

## The problem with one product

**For the adult market it is too cheap.** Not in price — in ingredients. Every
margarita machine rental in Austin runs the same bulk sweet-and-sour syrup:
corn syrup, citric acid, artificial colour, "natural and artificial flavour."
It is fine. It is also exactly what a household in Rob Roy or Barton Creek can
taste the difference from, and exactly the sort of ingredient list they read
labels to avoid.

Those customers are not price-sensitive. They are quality-sensitive. Charging
them $250 for syrup is not a bargain, it is a mismatch — and it means competing
on price with people who have more machines.

**For the kid market the ingredients are the problem.** Affluent Austin parents
are actively dye-averse. Red 40, Blue 1, Yellow 5 — the exact things that make a
blue raspberry slushie blue — are the things a lot of these parents specifically
avoid. Nobody in this category is offering an alternative.

---

## The idea: a quality ladder

Same machine. Same delivery, setup and pickup. Different mix.

| | **Classic** | **Fresh Press** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Base | Bulk mix, current recipe | Fresh-squeezed citrus, real fruit |
| Sweetener | As-is | Agave, or a zero-sugar option |
| Colour | As-is | From the fruit only. No dyes |
| Adult version | Margarita Lime | Skinny margarita — fresh lime, agave, no syrup |
| Kid version | Six current flavours | Real-fruit versions, no artificial colour |
| Price | $250 | Premium — see below |

The ladder works in **both** markets, which is the part that makes it more than
a niche upgrade:

- **Adults on Lake Austin** get a genuinely good skinny margarita instead of a
  syrup one.
- **Parents in Steiner and Belterra** get a birthday party drink with no
  artificial dye in it — which for a meaningful slice of that market is the
  whole decision.

---

## Why this is defensible

Anyone can buy a machine. Nobody in this market is squeezing limes.

Fresh preparation is genuinely hard to copy because it is *work*, not equipment.
A competitor with six machines and a warehouse cannot do it without changing
their whole operation. Two kids and a citrus press can.

It also converts the business from **competing on price** to **competing on
quality**, which is the only competition worth being in when the alternative is
a field of DR-0 websites all charging about the same.

And it gives an honest answer to the one hard question about this business:
*why would I pay two kids the same as a real rental company?* Because the two
kids squeezed the limes this morning.

---

## Pricing

Not a number to guess at — but the logic:

- Ingredient cost difference is real but small. Fresh limes for a large batch
  run maybe $30–50 more than syrup.
- The price difference should be much larger than the cost difference, because
  what is being sold is not limes, it is *made this morning by hand*.
- The Lake Austin / Westlake / Barton Creek market will not blink at a
  meaningful premium for that. They pay it everywhere else in their lives.
- Keep Classic at $250 so nothing is taken away from existing customers and the
  entry point stays honest.

**Decision for Mark:** the Fresh Press price, and whether the kid version is
priced the same as the adult one or lower.

## The thing to test before selling any of this

**Frozen drink machines rely on sugar and alcohol as antifreeze.** The mix
freezes at the right slushy consistency because of its sugar content. A low
sugar "skinny" recipe can freeze too hard — the machine labours, the paddles
strain, and instead of a slush you get a block.

This is not a reason to drop the idea. It is a reason to **test it thoroughly at
home before it is ever sold to a customer.** Things to work out:

- How low the sugar can go before the freeze fails
- Whether agave behaves like sugar in the tank
- Whether a zero-sugar sweetener works at all, or needs something else added
- How fresh juice separates or behaves over a four-hour party
- How long fresh mix keeps — likely same-day only, which is an operational
  change

Getting this wrong in front of a paying Lake Austin customer would cost more
than the idea is worth. Getting it right first is a genuine competitive moat,
because it is the part a competitor would have to redo from scratch.

---

## The operational change

Fresh means same-day prep. That is a real difference from opening a bottle.

- Limes get squeezed the morning of the party
- Mix does not sit in a garage for a week
- There is a shopping trip attached to every Fresh Press booking
- It probably needs a minimum notice period — a Fresh Press booking cannot be
  taken the night before

Worth deciding whether Fresh Press is offered every day or only on weekends when
there is time to prepare properly.

## The content play, which might be worth more than the product

This is where it gets good, because the *making* is far better content than the
drink.

1. **Read the ingredient list on the mix everyone else uses.** Out loud. Slowly.
   That single video is the entire positioning argument and it makes itself.
2. **Squeeze a hundred limes.** Satisfying, absurd, obviously real work. This is
   the shot that proves the whole claim.
3. **Blind taste test: syrup versus fresh.** Let people who do not know which is
   which pick. Post the result honestly even if it is close.
4. **Develop the recipe on camera over several videos.** Too sour, too sweet,
   too thin. Getting it wrong repeatedly is better content than getting it right
   once, and it is genuinely how recipes get made.
5. **The freeze test.** Find out how low the sugar can go before the machine
   fights back — on camera. This is real science with a real answer and it is
   the kind of thing a teacher shows a class.
6. **Colour without dye.** Show real fruit making real colour next to the
   artificial version. Visual, immediate, needs no explanation.
7. **Cost it out loud.** What limes cost, what syrup costs, why the fresh one is
   priced higher. That is the business lesson and the price justification in one
   video.

### Why the girls can own this

They can develop and make the **mix** — citrus, fruit, agave, ratios. That is
their recipe and their work.

They do not touch the tequila. The customer adds it at setup, exactly as now.

Which means they can be the genuine authority on the recipe while the alcohol
stays entirely a customer decision. That is a cleaner position than most adult
operators have, and it is true.

## What to call it

Not decided. The naming should make the difference obvious in three words on a
pricing page. Some directions:

- **Classic / Fresh Press**
- **House / Reserve**
- **Original / Hand-Squeezed**
- **The $250 one / The one where we squeeze the limes**

The last one is a joke, but it is also the clearest, and it is how the girls
would actually explain it.
