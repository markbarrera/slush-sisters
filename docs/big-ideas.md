# Bigger swings

The playbook in `marketing.md` is the day-to-day. This is the ambitious list —
ideas with a higher ceiling, some of which need a grown-up to make a phone call
first, and one of which needs a lawyer.

Nothing here is decided. Read it, argue about it, pick the ones you like.

---

## Things nobody else in this business has

### The Annual Report
A real printed annual report from a 10-year-old CEO. Revenue, cost per party,
cups poured, the loan balance still owed to Dad, a photo of the van, and a
one-page letter from Finley about the year. Then mail forty physical copies to
real people — investors, founders, the *Austin Monthly* editor, the Community
Impact newsdesk, big Texas brands.

*Why it works:* everyone in kid-entrepreneur content **claims** to run a real
business. A document with actual numbers is proof, and it is a physical object a
journalist can hold. One reply out of forty is a national story.
**~$60. One weekend a year.**

### The Open Ledger
A page on the site that updates after every rental: cups poured, parties served,
money in, money out, exact balance still owed on the machine. A counter that
only moves one way, in public, forever.

*Why it works:* it answers the only question a sceptic has, in four seconds, and
in three years the archive of that counter *is* the story.
**Free. Lives or dies on a monthly habit.**

### The Rejection Wall
A public list of every no. Brands that never replied. The party that said $250
was too much. The flavour that failed. Dated, unedited, no spin.

*Why it works:* it is the cheapest credibility there is, and it inverts what
everyone expects from a kid business. It also quietly pressures the brands on
the list — nobody wants to be the company publicly ignoring two little girls.
**Free. Never name a private customer.**

### Cup Number
Every cup ever poured is numbered, cumulatively, since day one. The count lives
on the van and on the site. Hosts get told their party's range — *"you were cups
5,140 to 5,266."* The 10,000th cup becomes an event.

*Why it works:* it makes a story out of ordinary work, and gives you a number
that only ever goes up.
**Free. Needs one honest tally sheet in the van.**

## Content franchises you could own for years

### Kids Review Ads
Rate real advertising. Billboards on the 620 drive, Super Bowl spots, the
packaging on the candy you buy for garnishes. Out of ten, with reasons, one
minute each.

*Why it works:* nothing to do with slushies, so it grows the audience past
people who want to rent a machine. And it is aimed squarely at marketers — who
share things compulsively, book corporate events, and decide brand
partnerships. Dad's whole professional world becomes a distribution channel.
**Free. You have to be allowed to genuinely dislike things.**

### The Disagreement Desk
Take opposite sides of a real business decision — buy a second machine, drop a
flavour, drive to Dripping Springs for one booking. Ninety seconds each. The
comments vote. You abide by the result and post what happened later.

*Why it works:* two sisters is a structural advantage most accounts do not have.
Voting turns viewers into people with a stake, and every vote guarantees a
follow-up post months later.
**Free. Money questions stay off the ballot.**

### Dad's Performance Review
Quarterly, on camera, the two owners formally review their driver. Punctuality,
van cleanliness, attitude at setup, whether he talked too much to a customer
again. Scored, with written feedback and a raise decision.

*Why it works:* it is the funniest true thing about this company, and it
reframes Dad from "the adult obviously running this" into a subordinate
character — which strengthens the premise instead of undermining it. Everyone
has a boss, so it travels well beyond Austin.
**Free. An hour a quarter. Dad has to actually take the notes.**

### Kid Business Radio
Interview other kid entrepreneurs. The 12-year-old who mows lawns, the girl
selling bracelets at the farmers market, kids in other states. Ask about money,
not hobbies: what did it cost, what did you make, what went wrong.

*Why it works:* almost no kid creator interviews anyone. It makes you the centre
of a category rather than one entry in it, and every guest's parents share it.
**Free. An adult has to book guests and get permission from their parents.**

## Turning customers into part of it

### Apprentice of the Day
Every booking includes one job for one kid at the party — usually the birthday
kid. A Slush Sisters shirt, a title, two minutes of training, and they work the
first fifteen minutes alongside you. They keep the shirt.

*Why it works:* it makes $250 an experience instead of a delivered object, and
it guarantees the host posts — a parent always shares a photo of their own kid
working a job. That post lands in exactly the neighbourhood the bookings come
from. It also solves the privacy problem at fancy houses, because the host is
the one filming.
**~$8 a shirt. Best value on this whole list.**

### The Party Report Card
Grade every party. Crowd energy, best costume, cups per guest, garnish
appreciation, did anyone say thank you. A letter grade and a handwritten note at
pickup.

*Why it works:* the vendor reviewing the customer is novel enough to be shared
on its own, and an A+ from the Slush Sisters becomes a small local status
object.
**Free. Positive only — nobody gets publicly graded badly.**

## Partnerships nobody would predict

### The Realtor Circuit
Frozen drinks at west Austin open houses. Approach the top listing agents in
Lakeway, Steiner and Westlake with a weekday-afternoon rate, on the condition
that the girls set up and stay the first hour.

*Why it works:* weekday afternoons are currently worth nothing. Realtors post
constantly, have budget, and their open houses are full of exactly the target
customer. One agent who likes you books monthly.
**Free to pitch. Ten conversations. This one changes revenue, not just reach.**

### Winter: sell to grown-ups, not to the cold

November to February is dead for kids' birthdays. It is the *busiest* month of
the year for office and adult holiday parties.

Same machine, same frozen margaritas, no new equipment, completely different
buyer. Office parties, HOA holiday events, neighbourhood progressive dinners,
teacher appreciation lunches — all clustered in the exact weeks the birthday
calendar goes quiet.

**Free to try. This is the winter business.**

### Hot cocoa — test it, do not build it

Worth knowing why this is smaller than it sounds: a frozen drink machine cannot
make hot cocoa, so it means buying equipment. And Austin winters are mild —
December highs average around 62°F, so demand is a short cold snap, not a
season.

Two venues make it worth *one cheap test*: **Bee Cave on Ice** (an outdoor rink
behind City Hall, mid-November to mid-January — two months of people standing
outside in the cold by choice) and the **Lakeway Trail of Lights**, which
already takes food vendors.

Spend about $150 on urns and try one event. Buy equipment only if people
actually queue.

### The Candy Company Play
You have been putting Sour Patch Watermelon on every cup since day one, unpaid,
unprompted, and it is the stated differentiator of the whole business. Write
that up as one page with photos and the cup count. Send it to the brand teams at
Mondelez, Haribo and Trolli.

*Why it works:* it is not a sponsorship request from a random account, it is
documented unpaid product integration with a story attached. Free candy improves
the economics; a feature from a national brand is reach you cannot buy.
**Free to send. Low odds, big payoff. Send it every year.**

### The Old Guy
Find the Austin margarita machine operator who has been doing it twenty years
and interview him. What broke, what he charges, what he wishes he had known,
what he makes of two kids entering his market.

*Why it works:* everyone treats competitors as competitors, which is exactly why
this lands. It is generous and confident, it gets you his audience and his
overflow bookings when he is double-booked, and "the veteran and the 10-year-old"
is a segment a TV producer can picture instantly.
**Free. One phone call.**

## Stunts that earn media

### Hottest Day of the Year
Austin's peak temperature day is a guaranteed annual news cycle — every station
sends a crew out for heat footage in August. On the day the forecast says 105°+,
set up somewhere public and give away free cups until the mix runs out. Tell the
stations that morning.

*Why it works:* you are handing a news desk the exact shot they are already
driving around looking for, on a day with no competing story.
**~$40 of mix. Needs permission for the spot. Never announce it in advance.**

### Now Hiring: Delivery Driver
Post a real job listing for the position Dad currently holds. Reports to Finley
Barrera, CEO, age 10. Requirements: clean driving record, able to lift 40
pounds, tolerance for being told what to do by children. Put it on LinkedIn.
Interview two or three real applicants on camera.

*Why it works:* it lands directly in Dad's professional world, where the sharing
is done by people with reach. The joke is legible in two seconds and it is also
literally true.
**Free. Do not actually hire anyone.**

### The Tequila Refusal
Publish a short, plain policy: the machine makes frozen limeade, adults add
their own tequila, and Slush Sisters will not take money from an alcohol brand.
Then when a liquor brand asks, post the decline.

*Why it works:* the tension between "margarita machine" and "run by an
8-year-old" is the most interesting thing about this company. Owning it out loud
is stronger than keeping the two apart — it defuses the only objection a school
or reporter would raise, and a public no to money is the most credible thing a
business can say.
**Free. Requires committing to the no. Entirely Dad's call.**

## Business model twists

### The Garnish Kit
The content audience will be national and cannot book a rental in Lakeway. Sell
them the one thing that ships: a small box of the six candy garnishes, matched
to the six flavours, with a card in your handwriting explaining which goes with
which. Site only, limited runs, sold out when it is sold out.

*Why it works:* it is the only way to make anything from a follower in Ohio, it
is built entirely from the differentiator you already own, and it turns a
business with a hard geographic ceiling into one with a product line.
**~$300 for a first run of 50, assembled at the kitchen table.**

### Chapters
Kids in other cities run their own Slush Sisters. Same name, same candy garnish
rule, same flat price, using a free playbook Finley and Harper write. No fees,
an application, and a rule set. Start with three, in three states.

*Why it works:* "a 10-year-old with franchisees in four states" is a national
headline that no amount of good content buys otherwise. Highest ceiling of
anything written here.
**Near free. Needs a lawyer's ten minutes first — licensing a name with rules
attached can trip franchise law, and other people's children working is its own
question. Year two, not year one.**

## Retracted: licensing the garnish

An earlier version of this list suggested selling the candy garnish system to
competing rental companies. **That was wrong and it has been removed.**

There is no IP in a sweet on a cup rim. Anyone can buy the same bag of Sour
Patch Kids. No competitor would pay for something a shopping trip replicates,
and pre-portioned kits is a thin commodity margin not worth the operational
drag.

The garnish is a **positioning and content asset** — it is why the cup gets
photographed and why people remember the brand. That is genuinely valuable. It
is simply not licensable, and the two things are easy to confuse.

What actually is defensible here, roughly in order:

1. The brand, and the girls themselves
2. The content archive, once it is a few years deep
3. The customer list and the rebooking relationship
4. **The freeze-behaviour knowledge**, if the Fresh Press work gets done —
   working out how low the sugar can go before a machine stalls takes a
   competitor months of ruined batches to rediscover. That is the closest thing
   to a trade secret this business could own, and a good reason to do that work
   properly.

The consumer **Garnish Kit** still stands, but for a different reason than
first given: a fan in Ohio buys it because it is *from the Slush Sisters*, not
because the sweets are special. Merchandise with a story, not intellectual
property.

The **overflow deal** also stands. It never depended on the garnish.

## If you only pick five

1. **Apprentice of the Day** — $8, start this weekend, improves the product and
   the content at once
2. **The Annual Report** — the one most likely to get a national outlet to
   notice
3. **Kids Review Ads** — the best repeatable format, aimed at the audience that
   can change everything
4. **The Open Ledger** — cheapest permanent asset, answers the sceptic's
   question forever
5. **The Realtor Circuit** — the only one that changes the money rather than the
   reach

**Sleeper:** Dad's Performance Review. Free, an hour a quarter, and the funniest
true thing about the company.
