The three numbers we will publish

Every rental company in Austin will tell you their drink is good. None of them will tell you what is in it or how they know. These are the numbers that make “good” checkable.

13.5
Brix in the finished drink
Brix is how much sugar is dissolved in the mix, measured with a refractometer. Below about 12 it freezes too hard for the machine to turn. Above about 16.5 it stops setting properly and slumps in the cup. 13.5 sits in the middle with room on both sides for a hot day.
16.9
Brix in the base we make
The tequila a grown-up adds waters the mix down by about a fifth. So the base starts sweeter and lands at 13.5. Finished = base × 0.8.
<9%
Alcohol in the finished drink
Alcohol fights freezing about 7.4× harder than sugar does. Past 9% no amount of sugar saves the texture. This one is a number for whoever is pouring.

Here is the part we did not expect. Measuring sugar in an alcoholic mix is genuinely hard — alcohol bends light differently than sugar, so the reading comes out wrong and you need correction tables to fix it. Harper and Finley only ever handle the base, before any alcohol goes in. So their reading needs no correction at all. The rule that keeps two kids away from tequila happens to give them a cleaner measurement than most bars have.

And here is what the arithmetic told us afterwards: Fresh Press needs two recipes, not one.

The base is built sweet on purpose, because the tequila a grown-up adds waters it down by about a fifth and lands it at 13.5. But nothing gets added to the kids’ tank — so that same base stays at 16.9, which is too sweet to freeze properly. It slumps in the cup instead of holding a shape.

Same limes, same agave, less of it. We did not know that until we did the math, and you can do them yourself in the base builder in the Lab.

Why it is $125 more when the limes cost $30

Because you are not buying limes.

We looked this up properly. The ingredient difference between bulk syrup and fresh-squeezed citrus, for one party’s worth of mix, is somewhere between $20 and $40. There is a bartender technique called super juice — you use the peels plus two acids you can buy in a tub — that gets the same sourness and the same lime smell out of about a seventh as many limes — nine instead of fifty-eight for a three-gallon batch. We are going to use it, and we are telling you we use it.

So the extra $125 is not the ingredients. It is the morning spent squeezing, the shopping trip, the mix that cannot sit in a garage for a week, the recipe testing that had to happen first, and the fact that nobody else within thirty miles will do it.

If that is not worth $125 to you, book the Classic. It is a good drink and it is what most of our parties are. We would rather tell you the honest difference than dress up a syrup drink.

Questions

Is the Classic bad, then?

No. It is the same mix every margarita machine company around here uses, and people love it. The difference is that Fresh Press is made by hand on the day and has nothing artificial in it. Both are real products. One costs more because it costs more to make.

Why five days’ notice?

Because fresh means fresh. There is a shopping trip attached to every Fresh Press booking, and the mix gets made the morning of your party rather than pulled off a shelf. Five days is what makes that possible without rushing it.

Can I have one tank Classic and one tank Fresh Press?

Ask us. The machine does not care — the two tanks are independent. We just have not decided how to price it yet, and we would rather answer you honestly than guess on a website.

Do you provide the alcohol?

No, and we never will. We make the mix, which is the part with no alcohol in it. A grown-up adds their own spirits once we have left. That is a rule we published on purpose, before anyone asked.

What does “no artificial dye” actually mean?

No Red 40, no Blue 1, no Yellow 5. The color comes from the fruit, which means a strawberry drink is a slightly duller pink than the bright one you are used to. That is what real strawberries look like. We will show you a photo before you book so there is no surprise.

What is a Tommy’s margarita?

The margarita most bars actually make when you ask for a good one. It drops the orange liqueur and the sour mix and uses just three things: tequila, fresh lime juice and agave. It was invented at Tommy’s in San Francisco and it is the reason a real margarita tastes like lime instead of like sweets.

Ours is the frozen version of that, and the grown-up adds their own tequila. That rule does not change.
