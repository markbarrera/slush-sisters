The lab

We keep testing this stuff until it works.

We thought you just poured syrup in and it turned into a slushie. It does not. Put in too little sugar and you get a big lump of ice that the machine cannot even turn. Put in too much and it stays a puddle. So now we measure everything, write it down, and keep it — even the times we got it completely wrong. Especially those, actually.

Sugar is not there to make it sweet

Well — it is. But that is not its main job. Sugar is what stops the drink freezing solid. Plain water turns into a rock at 32°F. Stir enough sugar into it and it will not freeze until it gets colder than that, so instead of a rock you get a slush.

Which means the “healthy” version of a slushie with the sugar taken out is not a healthy slushie. It is an ice cube. We know because we made one.

Try it: the freeze test

Slide it and see what our machine would do. The number is called Brix, which is just a fancy word for how much sugar is in there. 15 means 15 out of every 100 bits of the drink are sugar.

Why the sugar-free version is hard

This is the part that surprises people, and it is the reason we test before we sell anything. How sweet something tastes and how much it stops water freezing are two completely different properties. They only travel together in ordinary sugar because that is what everyone is used to.

Sort this table two ways

Tap Freezing power. Then tap Sweetness. The order jumps around completely, and that is the whole point — how sweet something tastes has almost nothing to do with how much it stops things freezing. Sugar is 100 in both columns, and everything else is compared to it.

Look at the two weird ones. Salt is not sweet at all, and it is nearly six times better than sugar at stopping things freezing — that is why people put salt on icy roads. And sucralose, the sweetener in diet drinks, is hundreds of times sweeter than sugar but does basically nothing to the freezing.

So a slushie sweetened only with sucralose would taste sweet and then freeze into a solid brick. We have not tried it yet. We think we know exactly what will happen, and we are going to film it happening.

Making colors without dye

Lots of parents would rather their kid did not drink Red 40 or Blue 1. Fair enough. You can get color out of actual plants instead — it is just harder, and some plants are much better at it than others. Here is what we have found out so far.

Sour drinks need tough colors

Hibiscus flowers, black carrots and purple sweet potatoes keep their color even in a really sour drink. Slushies are sour, so these are the ones that work.

Beets let you down

It looks amazing in the jug. Then it fades in the sun and goes a sad brownish color about an hour into the party. We would rather tell you that than find out at yours.

Cold makes colors last

Plant colors last about twice as long cold as they do warm. Good news: a frozen tank is cold. Bad news: a cup sitting on a sunny table is not.

The really hard one: blue raspberry

Here is a thing we did not know. Blue raspberry is not a fruit. Somebody made it up. There is no blue raspberry growing anywhere, so there is nothing blue to squeeze.

The only real answer we have found is a flower called butterfly pea. You soak it and the water goes deep blue — and then if you add lemon it turns pink and purple right in the cup, while you are holding it. We are testing it. If it works it is going to be the best thing at the party.

We found something out doing this

The two readouts above do not match, and at first we thought the calculator was broken.

It is not. A base built for grown-ups has to be extra sweet, because the tequila is going to water it down by about a fifth. But nobody waters down the kids’ tank. So the same mix that comes out perfect for the grown-ups comes out too sweet for the kids, and too sweet means it never really freezes — it just slumps in the cup.

So Fresh Press needs two recipes, not one. Same limes, same agave, different amount of agave. We did not know that this morning. It came out of doing the math, which is exactly what the math is for.

How we are actually going to measure it

Every number on this page is waiting on one ten-minute job nobody has done yet. Here is the plan, written down before we do it so we cannot quietly change it afterwards.

1. Get a refractometerabout $30
2. Measure the syrup we already use, straight out of the jug2 min
3. Measure a normal batch after mixing it with water2 min
4. Check that against what the calculator up there predicted1 min
5. Measure the agave and the lime juice3 min
6. Write all five numbers on this page, right or wrong—

Step 4 is the interesting one. If the real number and the predicted number do not match, the calculator is wrong and we will say so here rather than quietly fixing it. That is the whole point of writing the plan down first.

And then the part that actually takes time

Measuring is quick. Making the Fresh Press recipe work is not, because a real-fruit mix has to survive four hours in a tank on a Texas afternoon without separating, and the only way to find that out is to make it and watch it.

Fresh Press is bookable now with five days’ notice, and every batch gets measured before it goes in the van. If a batch does not come out right, it does not leave the house.

Things we got wrong

These stay up. If we only showed you the parts that worked, this would not be a lab, it would be an ad.

We guessed instead of measuring, and the calculator called us liars

The mix calculator further up this page used to have the syrup number built in, so you could not see it or change it. We had guessed that number. It was wrong — so the calculator confidently told people that our normal recipe, the one we have made dozens of times and which works fine, would come out as a solid block of ice.

It sat there like that for a week. Now the number is a box you can see and change, with a note saying we have not measured ours yet. The lesson: a guess hidden inside a machine still looks like a fact.

We said a video did well, from a screenshot

We were looking at another kid’s business account to learn from it, and we said one type of their video was their best kind. We were going off a screenshot, not the numbers.

Then we actually counted the views. That kind of video was one of their worst — about 3,900 views, when their best one had over a million. We had it backwards by about 250 times. The lesson: looking at something is not the same as counting it.

We made a slushie with no sugar in it

It froze into a lump the machine could not turn. We could hear the motor straining. That is the whole reason there is a freeze test at the top of this page — so nobody else has to learn it the way we did, with the noise.
